\title{Constraint Encoding and Penalty Weight Determine\\Quantum-Inspired
Solver Performance on a Real Crop-Allocation QUBO}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}
\IEEEauthorblockA{NGIA-QC 2026 --- Double-blind review}}

\maketitle
\thispagestyle{empty}

\begin{abstract}
When many farmers independently choose the same profitable crop, the combined
harvest depresses its price. We write this for 174 real parcels (1{,}464~ha)
in Jenin, Palestine as a 1{,}914-variable QUBO with market-clearing prices,
and benchmark two constraint encodings against two solvers over 20 seeds at a
matched wall-clock budget. Encoding, not solver, decides usability: under
one-hot encoding discrete simulated bifurcation leaves 130 of 174 parcels
invalid and earns 67\% less than annealing, while domain-wall encoding raises
its profit by 138\% and makes the two solvers indistinguishable. We then sweep
the domain-wall ordering penalty, a weight that published comparisons fix
without reporting, and find it inverts the apparent conclusion: annealing
loses 22\% at the conventional weight and gains 18\% at twice that value. A
myopic greedy plan loses \$10.5M by flooding the market. Both learned
components are reported as negative results.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
quantum-inspired optimisation, simulated bifurcation, QUBO, domain-wall
encoding, constraint encoding, crop planning
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Crop planning is an allocation problem constrained by a market signal no
individual farmer can observe. A farmer plants on last season's prices without
knowing what the neighbours intend; when many independently converge on the
same profitable crop, the combined harvest depresses its price. Every decision
is individually rational and the aggregate outcome is a collective loss, a
coordination failure that bites hardest where output cannot be held back until
prices recover. Producer prices in Palestine move sharply year to year: across
2010--2023 the largest single-year change in the FAOSTAT series~\cite{faostat}
ranges from 14\% for wheat to 60\% for oranges, and the two crops in our set
that store well, wheat and olives, sit at the bottom of that range at 14\% and
23\%. Recent crop-planning models therefore treat market absorption as a
binding constraint rather than assuming prices are exogenous to the
plan~\cite{li2025,hou2026}.

Allocating $P$ parcels among $C$ crops admits $C^P$ candidate assignments and
does not decompose: because revenue depends on aggregate production, the best
crop for one parcel depends on the choices made for all the others. Problems
of this form map onto quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation
(QUBO)~\cite{lucas2014,glover2019}, the input format shared by quantum
annealers and by a growing family of quantum-inspired classical solvers. Among
the latter, simulated bifurcation (SB)~\cite{goto2021} integrates the
classical Hamiltonian dynamics of nonlinear oscillators. All variables update
simultaneously, so SB parallelises where sequential methods such as simulated
annealing~\cite{kirkpatrick1983} cannot, and it has cut time-to-solution
substantially against annealing hardware on large benchmarks~\cite{zeng2024}.

Passing a discrete allocation problem to any such solver first requires an
\emph{encoding}: a representation of each parcel's crop choice in binary
variables, together with a mechanism enforcing that exactly one crop is
selected. One-hot encoding imposes this through a quadratic penalty over all
pairs of binaries within a parcel. Domain-wall encoding~\cite{chancellor2019}
instead constrains a chain of binaries to be non-increasing, replacing the
dense per-parcel penalty with a local penalty between adjacent binaries.
Encoding is ordinarily treated as an implementation detail, subordinate to the
choice of solver. On the instance studied here it is not: it decides whether
the quantum-inspired solver returns a usable plan at all.

A second decision is buried one level deeper. The domain-wall ordering penalty
carries a weight that published comparisons fix once and do not report. We
show that this weight determines not only the size of the encoding effect but
its \emph{sign} for one of the two solvers, so a single-weight study can reach
the opposite conclusion from a swept one.

Our contributions are threefold. First, a $2\times2$ encoding-by-solver
benchmark on a real 1{,}914-variable agricultural QUBO, over 20 seeds at a
verified wall-clock budget, in which domain-wall encoding raises discrete
simulated bifurcation's profit by 138\% and renders the two solvers
statistically indistinguishable. Second, a sweep of the domain-wall ordering
penalty across five weights showing that the conventional value sits in a
region where annealing appears to lose 22\%, while at twice that weight
domain-wall improves both solvers; the apparent conclusion inverts with an
unreported hyperparameter. Third, an evaluation of every learned component
against a trivial baseline, in which an LSTM price forecaster fails to beat
persistence and a Random Forest suitability model is shown to carry almost no
parcel-level discriminating power, both reported rather than omitted.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{AI-Based Crop Recommendation and Price Forecasting}

Random Forest, an ensemble of bagged decision trees, remains a strong baseline
for tabular agricultural data because it absorbs nonlinear, heterogeneous
features without extensive preprocessing~\cite{breiman2001}. Dey et
al.~\cite{dey2024} reached classification accuracy above 99\% for crop
recommendation with XGBoost on soil and climatic features, but their pipeline
stops at suitability: with no forecasting or allocation stage, a crop flagged
suitable may still be a poor choice if the market is saturated. Saha et
al.~\cite{saha2025} combined satellite land mapping, machine-learning crop and
yield prediction above 95\% accuracy, and irrigation control; their LSTM
forecasts vegetation area rather than prices, so it cannot signal oversupply
before it occurs.

Because the allocation objective is driven by prices, the quality of the price
input matters as much as the optimiser; Li et al.~\cite{li2025} address this by
feeding neural-network forecasts into theirs. Long Short-Term Memory
networks~\cite{hochreiter1997} are widely applied to agricultural price series
for the same purpose. Their benefit is sensitive to record length, and a
governorate-level history offers only a few decades of annual observations per
crop. Whether a learned forecaster improves on a naive baseline at this data
scale is an empirical question rather than a given, and we treat it as one.

\subsection{Optimisation: Classical and Quantum-Inspired}

Crop allocation was traditionally posed as linear programming, but recent work
treats it as a nonlinear problem integrating prediction and market effects. Li
et al.~\cite{li2025} combined simulated annealing with a genetic algorithm
across 34 plots in northern China, constraining production by regional
market-absorption capacity and farm water supply, and beat standalone GA, SA,
PSO and ACO by 5--10 percentage points of profit increment. Hou et
al.~\cite{hou2026} paired particle swarm optimisation with simulated annealing
and Monte Carlo simulation, lowering prices when production exceeds demand,
and raised expected profit by 12.6\% over a baseline PSO.

Quantum-inspired approaches offer a different route. Zeng et
al.~\cite{zeng2024} benchmarked quantum-annealing-inspired algorithms against
a D-Wave annealer on Max-Cut instances of up to 20{,}000 nodes: ballistic SB
cut time-to-solution nearly fifty-fold on small and Chimera-structured graphs,
and discrete SB was fastest on large ones. On dense QUBO instances, Kim et
al.~\cite{kim2025} found a hybrid quantum-annealing solver roughly
6{,}561$\times$ faster than the best classical solver. Within agriculture,
Verma et al.~\cite{verma2025} paired a quantum convolutional network with QAOA
and reported speed-ups of up to 3.5$\times$.

Encoding has been studied separately from solvers. Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2021} compared domain-wall against one-hot on D-Wave annealing
hardware across three problems and found domain-wall superior on every metric,
with none favouring one-hot. Berwald et al.~\cite{berwald2022} proved
domain-wall optimal in binaries per discrete variable, showed its dynamics
freeze later on hardware, and conjectured that the advantage extends to
gate-model and quantum-inspired algorithms.

Two gaps follow. First, none of the agricultural studies formulates allocation
as a QUBO with market-clearing prices for Ising-type solvers. Second, the
encoding comparisons were run on annealing hardware and abstract problems,
within a single solver class, at one ordering-penalty weight. Whether the
conjecture of Berwald et al.\ holds for a quantum-inspired solver on a real
allocation problem, and whether the comparison is stable in the penalty
weight, have not been tested. Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarises the studies
reviewed.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Learned Components}

In 5-fold stratified cross-validation the Random Forest reaches
$0.9958\pm0.0021$ accuracy on the real rows against a majority-class baseline
of 0.0526, and $0.8940\pm0.0297$ on the synthetic rows, which measures
fidelity to the EcoCrop envelopes rather than predictive skill. Feature
importances are rainfall 0.275, humidity 0.174, K 0.150, P 0.134, temperature
0.092, N 0.091 and pH 0.083.

These figures overstate what the model contributes here. Within Jenin, soil pH
spans only 7.45--7.80 and available nitrogen 87--138~kg/ha across the 174
parcels, a range narrower than almost any split the forest learned on a global
training set. In consequence the 90 distinct soil profiles present in the data
collapse to between 2 and 12 distinct suitability values per crop. Parcel-level
suitability is therefore close to a per-crop constant modulated by a
three-valued land-use factor, and the model should not be read as
discriminating between individual parcels.

The optimiser requires a price for each crop. To decide whether that price
should be forecast or taken from recent history, we compared an
LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997} of 24 hidden units and 3{,}481 parameters against
four naive baselines under walk-forward validation, with the split made before
normalisation (Table~\ref{tab:lstm}).

forecasting skill.

Table~\ref{tab:bench} and Fig.~\ref{fig:encoding} give the results at
$M_{\mathrm{wall}}=1$. Under one-hot encoding dSB leaves 130 of 174 parcels
invalid and earns 67\% less than SA ($p<10^{-7}$): its continuous dynamics
bifurcate variables independently, and the dense per-parcel penalty does not
reliably leave exactly one positive. Domain-wall encoding raises dSB profit by
138\% ($p<10^{-7}$), after which the two solvers do not differ ($-1.5\%$,
$p=0.78$). At this penalty weight domain-wall \emph{lowers} SA profit by 22\%
($p<10^{-7}$). Section~\ref{sec:mwall} shows that this last result is an
artefact of the weight.

The greedy plan loses \$10.47M. Assigning every parcel its best-margin crop
concentrates production far beyond $D_j$; even when unsaleable surplus is
discarded rather than sold at the negative prices a linear demand curve
implies beyond $2D_j$, it still loses \$7.51M. This is the coordination
failure the formulation exists to avoid.

The ordering penalty is a free weight, and published comparisons fix it
without reporting a sweep. Table~\ref{tab:mwall} and Fig.~\ref{fig:mwall} show its effect over
$M_{\mathrm{wall}}\in\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}$, 20 seeds per point, against the
one-hot references of \$6.05M (SA) and \$2.04M (dSB) from the same experiment.

Profit rises monotonically with the weight for both solvers until it saturates
near $M_{\mathrm{wall}}=2$. Invalid parcels fall with it, from 23.0 (SA) and
20.3 (dSB) at the weakest weight through 5.0 and 3.4 at $M_{\mathrm{wall}}=1$
to 0.1 and 0.0 at $M_{\mathrm{wall}}=2$, where the seed variance also
collapses to $\pm0.03$M: a strong chain penalty does not merely permit valid
solutions, it makes the landscape tractable. Domain-wall exceeds one-hot for
dSB at every weight tested, by 67\% at the weakest and 226\% at the strongest.
For SA the comparison changes sign: a 45\% deficit at $M_{\mathrm{wall}}=0.25$,
17\% at the conventional value of 1, and an 18\% \emph{advantage} at 2 and
above.

Two conclusions follow. The domain-wall advantage reported on annealing
hardware~\cite{chen2021} does extend to a quantum-inspired solver on a real
allocation problem, confirming the conjecture of Berwald et
al.~\cite{berwald2022}, and at an adequate weight it extends to annealing as
well. But the conclusion a single-weight study reaches depends on which weight
it picked: at the value used in Section~\ref{sec:results}-B the same experiment
reports that domain-wall harms annealing. The weight belongs in the
experimental protocol, not in the defaults.

\subsection{Water and the Budget-Feasible Plan}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_water.pdf}
\caption{Irrigation demand against the water charge $\lambda$ (SA, one-hot,
50 seeds; bars are one standard deviation). Note the symmetric-log axis.}
\label{fig:water}
\end{figure}

PCBS reports 1.0~Mm$^3$/yr of agricultural abstraction from licensed
Palestinian wells in Jenin governorate, against 5.0~Mm$^3$ domestic and
6.0~Mm$^3$ total~\cite{pcbswater}. Every plan in Table~\ref{tab:bench} uses
2.3--9.0~Mm$^3$, so the licensed allocation binds hard.

Sweeping the water charge (Fig.~\ref{fig:water}) traces a demand curve that
falls monotonically from $6.37\pm0.37$~Mm$^3$ at $\lambda=0$ to
$2.57\pm0.30$~Mm$^3$ at \$3/m$^3$, then flattens: demand never falls below
2.54~Mm$^3$ at any charge up to \$20/m$^3$, still 2.5 times the licensed
budget. Pricing cannot reach this constraint. Above $\lambda\approx1$ the
formulation is also numerically degenerate, because once the water term
dominates the normalising coefficient the profit differences fall below the
fixed penalty weights, so only small $\lambda$ is interpretable.

The budget is therefore imposed directly, by fallowing parcels in ascending
order of margin per cubic metre until the plan fits, applied identically to
every arm including greedy. The resulting budget-feasible profits are the last
column of Table~\ref{tab:bench}. They fall into a narrow \$1.27--1.75M band,
cultivating only 9--47 of the 174 parcels, and the encoding difference for SA
becomes insignificant ($+3.2\%$, $p=0.31$) while for dSB it persists
($+32.7\%$, $p<10^{-4}$). Under the licensed allocation the problem is small
enough that encoding and solver choice largely stop mattering. Greedy reaches
\$1.37M under the same constraint: forced to leave most land fallow, it can no
longer flood the market, so the water limit incidentally performs the
coordination that the optimiser performs deliberately.

This deserves stating plainly rather than leaving implicit: at Jenin's
licensed allocation the problem is small enough that a trivial heuristic is
competitive, and the optimisation machinery earns little. It does not follow
that the encoding result is contingent on that. The result is a property of a
1{,}914-variable QUBO with ten 174-node cliques, not of Jenin's water
allocation: any allocation problem of comparable size and coupling density
presents the same encoding choice, and the water constraint shrinks this
instance without shrinking that class. The agricultural case is a testbed that
happens to be real, and the two findings bind at different scales, which is why
the unconstrained comparison and the budget-feasible plan are reported
separately rather than merged.

\subsection{Price Robustness}

The optimum is degenerate: inverse demand equalises marginal revenue across
crops, so many parcel-level plans reach similar profit and two runs differing
only in seed agree on 11.5\% of parcels. We therefore compare crop mix by
area, as $1-$ total variation distance, against a seed-control floor of 94.6\%
(50 seeds each).

asymmetry is economically sensible: a uniform rise scales revenues together and
barely changes relative margins, whereas a fall pushes marginal crops below
cost and forces reallocation toward low-cost perennials.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Market size $D_j$ is an assumption and the linear demand curve carries no
meaning beyond $Q_j=2D_j$, so every absolute profit reported here inherits that
assumption. The encoding and solver comparisons do not: both encodings are
built from the same instance, so a different $D_j$ rescales all four arms
together and leaves their ordering, and the percentage differences between
them, intact. The agricultural magnitudes are a demonstration; the
computational comparison does not rest on them. Climate is a single grid cell; the suitability
model has almost no parcel-level resolution, for the reasons in
Section~\ref{sec:results}-A; phosphorus and potassium are sampled, not
measured; SoilGrids values are model estimates; and yields are national
series rescaled to regional open-field levels. The PCBS figure covers
licensed wells only, excluding springs, purchased supply, wastewater reuse
and unlicensed abstraction, so it is a lower bound and the budget-feasible
plans are conservative. Reported profits are of decoded plans, so a solver
leaving many blocks outside the valid subspace is scored after an implicit
argmax repair. Wall-clock matching is enforced to 15\%, not exactly, on a
host that showed threefold timing variation. At 1{,}914 variables with ten
174-node cliques the instance exceeds the largest fully connected problem
directly embeddable on current annealing hardware ($K_{150}$ on
Pegasus~\cite{dwaveembed}), so this compares classical against
quantum-inspired solvers, not quantum hardware.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

On a real 1{,}914-variable crop-allocation QUBO, constraint encoding decided
whether a quantum-inspired solver was usable at all, and the weight of the
domain-wall ordering penalty decided the sign of the effect for annealing.
The domain-wall advantage therefore does extend from annealing hardware to
quantum-inspired solvers, as Berwald et al.~\cite{berwald2022} conjectured,
but any single-weight comparison risks reporting its opposite. Held to the
licensed agricultural water available in Jenin, every configuration converges
to \$1.3--1.8M on a sixth of the land, and the choice of encoding and solver
largely ceases to matter: the computational question and the agricultural one
bind at different scales. Future work should tune the ordering penalty per
solver rather than globally, measure freezing behaviour to test the mechanism
proposed by Berwald et al., and obtain parcel-level soil and crop-level demand
data adequate to the allocation question.
